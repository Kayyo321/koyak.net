# Registration Night — a visual philosophy

A print is proof that something was aligned. Registration Night is the aesthetic of the
alignment itself: the crosshairs, tick rings, step wedges, and trim marks that a press
operator sees and the public never does, promoted to the subject's equal. One strong
figure sits at the center of a field of instruments, and the instruments are drawn with
the same meticulous, almost devotional care as the figure — a craftsman's hand that has
inked ten thousand plates and still measures twice.

Space is circular and calibrated. A single ring defines the world; everything outside it
is margin, and the margin is where the measurements live. Ticks fall every two degrees,
heavier every ten, and the heavier ones carry a whisper of a numeral. Nothing is placed
by feel: radii are fractions of the plate, offsets are whole units of the dot pitch. The
work should look like it took countless hours, because every interval was counted.

Color is withheld. The plate is monochrome — light and its absence — so that ink can be
chosen later, at the press. Tone is described by a step wedge of eight patches along the
lower margin, the same way a printer proves density: a quiet admission that the image is
made of measurable quantities.

Lines are hairline and never decorative. A crosshair marks a center; a dashed arc marks a
tolerance; a bracket marks a crop. Text is clinical and tiny — an index, a coordinate, a
plate number — set in a thin face and never larger than it needs to be read by someone
leaning close. This is master-level restraint: the labor shows in precision, not in volume.

The figure and the instruments must interlock without touching: clear separation, generous
margins, nothing off the sheet, nothing overlapping. The result should read as a page from
the logbook of an imaginary discipline, a painstaking record that something ephemeral was
held still long enough to be printed.
